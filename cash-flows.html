Statement of Cash Flows

Lectures 23–24 — Indirect method, operating/investing/financing classification & free cash flow

L 23–24

Profit ≠ Survival

The Paradox: A company can report record profits on the income statement and still run out of cash. Net income tells you what was earned, not what was collected. You pay employees, suppliers, and lenders with cash, not net income.

Home Depot (1985)

Profitable on paper, came within weeks of bankruptcy. Rapid expansion consumed all cash despite strong earnings.

General Motors (2009)

Reported profits but exhausted liquidity. Filed Chapter 11 with billions in unpayable obligations.

Key insight: Accrual accounting records economic events, not cash exchanges. The SCF bridges the gap.

Three Questions the SCF Answers

1. Can the company pay its bills?

Does the business generate enough cash from operations to cover its obligations?

2. Where is the cash coming from?

Operations? Selling assets? Borrowing? Each source tells a different story about health.

3. Where is the cash going?

Investing in growth? Paying down debt? Returning capital to shareholders? The pattern reveals strategy.

Cash and Cash Equivalents

The SCF explains changes in cash and cash equivalents:

  • Cash on hand and in bank accounts
  • Cash equivalents: short-term, highly liquid investments
  • Easily convertible to a known amount of cash
  • Initial maturities of three months or less

Examples: Money market accounts, Treasury bills, commercial paper

Buying/selling cash equivalents is part of managing cash — they’re treated as cash itself, not as investing activities.

Back to the Equation

The SCF connects directly to the balance sheet: A = L + SE. The ending cash balance on the SCF equals the Cash line on the balance sheet. Every non-cash adjustment in the indirect method maps to another balance sheet or income statement item.

Two Methods, Same Answer

Direct Method

Cash from customers$X
Cash to suppliers(X)
Cash to employees(X)
Interest paid(X)
CFO$X

More transparent, but data-intensive.

Indirect Method

Net Income$X
+ Non-cash expensesX
− Non-cash income(X)
± Working capital Δ±X
CFO$X

Simpler. Used by ~98% of companies.

Key insight: Both methods produce the exact same CFO. They differ only in presentation. CFI and CFF are identical under both methods.

+ Increase in current liabilities (A/P, accrued exp.)X

Working Capital Adjustment Rules

Current Assets

(A/R, Inventory, Prepaid)

ChangeCashAdjust
Increase ↑Used− Subtract
Decrease ↓Freed+ Add

Current Liabilities

(A/P, Accrued Exp., Unearned Rev.)

ChangeCashAdjust
Increase ↑Kept+ Add
Decrease ↓Spent− Subtract

Key insight: Opposites — asset up → subtract from NI. Liability up → add to NI. The indirect method “undoes” accrual accounting to get back to cash.

Non-Cash & Non-Operating Adjustments

Add Back Non-Cash Expenses

  • + Depreciation — reduces NI but no cash leaves
  • + Amortization — same logic as depreciation
  • + Stock-based compensation — expense recognized, no cash paid

Remove Non-Operating Gains/Losses

  • − Gain on sale of asset — full proceeds go to CFI
  • + Loss on sale of asset — full proceeds go to CFI
  • − Unrealized gains — no cash involved

Important: Gains/losses on asset sales create a “double-counting” problem. The gain is in NI (CFO), but the full sale proceeds appear in CFI. Remove the gain from CFO so the cash is only counted once.

Back to the Equation

The indirect method starts with net income (which flows into SE via retained earnings) and adjusts for changes in A and L to arrive at the change in Cash (an asset). Every adjustment maps back to A = L + SE.

Cash Flow from Operations (CFO)

Cash generated or used by the company’s core business activities.

Inflows (+)

  • Cash collected from customers
  • Interest received
  • Dividends received

Outflows (−)

  • Cash paid to suppliers
  • Cash paid to employees (wages)
  • Interest paid
  • Income taxes paid
  • Operating lease payments

Cash Flow from Investing (CFI)

Cash spent on or received from long-term assets and investments.

Inflows (+)

  • Sale of PP&E
  • Sale of investments
  • Collection of notes receivable

Outflows (−)

  • Purchase of PP&E (CapEx)
  • Purchase of investments
  • Lending (notes receivable)

Cash Flow from Financing (CFF)

Cash from or returned to debt holders and equity holders.

Inflows (+)

  • Issuing common/preferred stock
  • Borrowing (bonds, loans)

Outflows (−)

  • Repaying debt principal
  • Paying dividends
  • Repurchasing (buying back) shares
  • Finance lease principal payments

Leases on the SCF

Finance Lease

Like a purchase with a loan — payment is split:

  • Interest portion → CFO
  • Principal portion → CFF

+ Add back ROU amortization (non-cash expense)

Operating Lease

Like a rental — single treatment:

  • Entire payment → CFO

No split needed — cash effect matches expense

Non-Cash Disclosures

Significant transactions involving no cash are excluded from the SCF body but must be disclosed in supplemental notes:

  • Converting debt to equity
  • Acquiring assets via lease inception
  • Issuing stock for an acquisition
  • Declaring stock dividends
=
Back to the Equation

CFO items affect current assets and current liabilities (working capital). CFI items affect long-term assets. CFF items affect long-term liabilities and stockholders’ equity. Each section maps to a region of A = L + SE.

Free Cash Flow

Free Cash Flow = CFO − Capital Expenditures

What is it?

Cash available after maintaining the asset base. Not a GAAP line item — widely used by analysts. Derived from the SCF: CFO minus CapEx (from the investing section).

Uses of Positive FCF

  • Repay debt
  • Pay dividends
  • Buy back shares
  • Fund new investments

Key insight: Positive FCF means the company generates cash beyond what it needs to sustain operations — a key indicator of financial flexibility.

Why FCF Matters

Valuation

Analysts value companies by discounting future FCF. A company is worth the cash it will generate — not its accounting earnings.

Sustainability

A company paying $2B in dividends with $1B in FCF is borrowing to pay shareholders — unsustainable.

Earnings Quality

NI can be managed through estimates. FCF is harder to fake — cash either came in or it didn’t.

Debt Capacity

Lenders assess FCF to determine how much debt a company can service. More FCF = more borrowing power.

What Cash Flow Patterns Tell You

CFO CFI CFF Likely Profile
+−−Healthy mature: generates cash, invests, returns capital
+−+Growth: operations + borrowing fund expansion
++−Restructuring: selling assets, paying down debt
−−+Startup: burning cash, financed by investors
−+−Distressed: selling assets to stay afloat

FCF = CFO − CapEx. It measures cash available after sustaining the asset base. Companies with strong FCF have flexibility to grow equity (retained earnings), reduce liabilities (repay debt), or return capital — all moving parts of A = L + SE.

Example 1: Working Capital Effects

ABC Corp sells chairs at $3 each, cost $2 each. Trace how credit sales and inventory changes affect the indirect method.

Step 1: Sells 60 chairs for cash ($180) and 40 on credit ($120). Buys 100 chairs for $200 cash.

Income Statement

Revenue300
COGS(200)
NI100

Direct Method

Cash from cust.180
Cash to suppl.(200)
CFO(20)

Indirect Method

Net Income100
− ΔA/R(120)
CFO(20)

Logic: A/R increased by $120 (asset up → subtract). Revenue included $120 that wasn’t collected as cash.

Step 2: Same sales. Also collects $120 of prior A/R. Also builds inventory (buys 120 chairs, sells 100).

Income Statement

Revenue300
COGS(200)
NI100

Direct Method

Cash from cust.420
Cash to suppl.(240)
CFO180

Indirect Method

Net Income100
+ ΔA/R (decrease)120
− ΔInv (increase)(40)
CFO180

Logic: A/R decreased by $120 (collected last period’s credit sale → add). Inventory increased by $40 (bought 120 but only sold 100 → subtract).

Step 3: Same sales. Buys 100 chairs on credit (A/P increases by $200).

Income Statement

Revenue300
COGS(200)
NI100

Direct Method

Cash from cust.300
Cash to suppl.0
CFO300

Indirect Method

Net Income100
+ ΔA/P (increase)200
CFO300

Logic: A/P increased by $200 (liability up → add). The company received inventory but hasn’t paid cash yet.

Example 2: Depreciation & Asset Sale

ABC Corp sells 100 chairs ($300 revenue, $200 COGS) plus faces depreciation and sells an asset.

Step 1: Annual depreciation on warehouse is $40.

Income Statement

Revenue300
COGS(200)
Depreciation(40)
NI60

Direct Method

Cash from cust.300
Cash to suppl.(200)
CFO100

Indirect Method

Net Income60
+ Depreciation40
CFO100

Logic: Depreciation reduced NI by $40 but no cash left the company. Add it back to convert NI to CFO.

Step 2: Sells old delivery truck (BV = $20) for $35 cash. Gain on sale = $15.

Income Statement

Operating NI60
Gain on sale15
NI75

Direct / Full SCF

CFO100
Sale proceeds35
CFI35

Indirect Method

Net Income75
+ Depreciation40
− Gain on sale(15)
CFO100
Sale proceeds35
CFI35

The double-counting problem: The $15 gain is in NI (CFO section). But the full $35 of cash proceeds goes to CFI. Removing the gain from CFO ensures the cash is only counted once — in CFI where the investing activity occurred.

Example 3: Financing Activities & SBC

ABC Corp issues shares, borrows, pays dividends, and grants stock compensation.

Step 1: Issues stock for $50. Borrows $80 (loan). Pays $20 interest. Pays $30 dividends.

Indirect Method (Financing Section)

+ Depreciation40
CFO120
CFI0
Stock issued50
Loan proceeds80
Dividends paid(30)
CFF100
Total CF220

Note: Interest paid is in CFO (operating). Dividends paid and debt/equity issuances are in CFF (financing).

Step 2: Grants $25 of stock-based compensation to employees.

Indirect Method (SBC Adjustment)

Net Income55
+ Depreciation40
+ Stock-based comp.25
CFO120

Logic: SBC is an expense on the income statement (reduces NI) but no cash was paid. Add it back, just like depreciation.

Combine everything. Company has: NI = $150, Depreciation = $40, SBC = $25, Gain on sale = $10, ΔA/R = +$30, ΔInventory = −$15, ΔA/P = +$20, CapEx = ($80), Sale proceeds = $50, Shares issued = $60, Dividends = ($25), Beginning cash = $100.
