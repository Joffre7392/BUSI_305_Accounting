Stockholders' Equity

Lectures 19–20 — Equity components, dividends, buybacks & real company analysis

L 19-20

What Is Stockholders' Equity?

Stockholders' equity is the residual claim on a company's assets after all liabilities have been settled. It represents what shareholders would theoretically receive if the company liquidated all assets and paid all debts.

Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders' Equity

Rearranged: SE = A − L. Equity is not a pile of cash — it is a residual, the net of everything the company owns minus everything it owes.

The Five Components

Component What It Represents Normal Balance
Common Stock Par value × shares issued Credit
APIC Excess over par received from shareholders Credit
Retained Earnings Cumulative net income minus cumulative dividends Credit
AOCI Unrealized gains/losses that bypass the income statement Credit (or Debit)
Treasury Stock Shares repurchased — contra equity (reduces SE) Debit

Capital Transactions vs Operations

Changes in equity come from two fundamentally different sources:

Capital Transactions

Transactions with owners

  • Issue shares → increases Common Stock & APIC
  • Pay dividends → decreases Retained Earnings
  • Repurchase shares → increases Treasury Stock (or reduces paid-in capital)

Operations

Business activities

  • Net Income → increases Retained Earnings
  • Net Loss → decreases Retained Earnings
  • OCI items → flow into AOCI

Retained Earnings — The Bridge

Ending R/E = Beginning R/E + Net Income − Dividends

Retained Earnings is the account that bridges the income statement and the balance sheet. Net income from the I/S flows into R/E on the B/S each period, and dividends declared flow out.

Important: R/E ≠ Cash. A company can have $10B in Retained Earnings and very little cash — the earnings were reinvested in equipment, inventory, acquisitions. R/E tells you how much profit was kept in the business, not where it went.

OCI and AOCI

Other Comprehensive Income (OCI) captures gains and losses that bypass the income statement under GAAP. These items are considered too volatile or unrealized to include in net income.

OCI Item Example
Unrealized gains/losses on AFS securities Bond portfolio value rises but not yet sold
Foreign currency translation adjustments Translating a foreign subsidiary's financials
Pension adjustments Changes in projected benefit obligation
Cash flow hedge gains/losses Effective portion of qualifying hedges

Key insight: AOCI accumulates OCI over time, just like Retained Earnings accumulates Net Income. OCI is a flow (this period); AOCI is a stock (cumulative).

Authorized vs Issued vs Outstanding

Authorized ≥ Issued = Outstanding + Treasury

Authorized

Maximum shares the company can issue (set in charter)

Issued

Shares actually sold to investors at some point

Outstanding

Issued shares currently in investors' hands

Only outstanding shares vote and receive dividends. Treasury shares have been repurchased and sit with the company — they have no voting or dividend rights.

Key insight: Under the retirement method, repurchased shares are cancelled, so Issued = Outstanding. Under the cost (treasury) method, Issued > Outstanding by the number of treasury shares.

Back to the Equation

Stock issuance increases both A (+Cash) and SE (+Common Stock, +APIC). The equation grows on both sides.

Dividends decrease A (−Cash) and SE (−R/E). The equation shrinks on both sides.

Buybacks decrease A (−Cash) and SE (−Treasury Stock or −Common Stock/APIC/R/E). Same shrinkage.

Net Income increases A (through revenue) and SE (through R/E). A = L + SE always holds.

Par Value vs No-Par Issuance

Example: A company issues 1,000 shares at $25/share. Total cash received: $25,000.

Par Value ($1/share)

Cash 25,000
Common Stock 1,000
APIC 24,000

Par absorbs $1 × 1,000 = $1,000. The remaining $24,000 goes to APIC.

No-Par Value

Cash 25,000
Common Stock 25,000

Everything goes into Common Stock. No APIC needed.

Key insight: Par value splits proceeds into two buckets. No-par puts everything in one. Total equity is the same either way — $25,000.

Why Does Par Value Still Exist?

Historical Purpose

  • Legal capital — minimum equity cushion for creditors
  • Creditor protection — dividends cannot reduce equity below legal capital
  • Minimum issue price — shares could not be sold below par

Modern Reality

  • Apple's par value: $0.00001
  • Most companies: $0.01 or less
  • Some states still require it; others don't

Key insight: Par value is an accounting and legal artifact, not a measure of what the stock is worth. Market price and par value are completely unrelated.

Stock Issued for Non-Cash Consideration

When stock is issued in exchange for assets or services instead of cash, record the transaction at the fair value of the consideration received (or the fair value of the stock issued, if more readily determinable).

Example: Issue 2,000 shares ($1 par) for equipment worth $50,000:

Equipment 50,000
Common Stock 2,000
APIC 48,000

Key insight: The asset side gets Equipment instead of Cash, but the equity side works exactly the same way — par goes to Common Stock, excess to APIC.

Cash vs Stock Dividends

Question Cash Dividend Stock Dividend
Does an asset leave? Yes (cash exits) No
Total equity changes? Decreases No change
Retained Earnings? Decreases Decreases
Creates a liability? Yes (Div Payable) No (Stock Div Distributable is equity)
Shares outstanding? No change Increases

Key insight: Cash dividend = a transfer between the company and shareholders. Stock dividend = a transfer within equity accounts — nothing leaves the company.

Cash Dividend — Three Key Dates

Example: $2/share on 5,000 outstanding shares = $10,000 total dividend.

1. Declaration Date

The board declares the dividend. A liability is created.

Retained Earnings 10,000
Dividends Payable 10,000

2. Record Date

No journal entry. This date determines which shareholders of record receive the dividend.

3. Payment Date

Cash is paid to shareholders. The liability is settled.

Dividends Payable 10,000
Cash 10,000

Stock Dividends — Large vs Small

Setup: 10 shares outstanding, $1 par, $2 market price.

Large Stock Dividend (>20–25%)

100% dividend = 10 new shares. Recorded at par value.

Retained Earnings 10
Common Stock 10

10 new shares × $1 par = $10

Small Stock Dividend (<20–25%)

10% dividend = 1 new share. Recorded at market value.

Retained Earnings 2
Common Stock 1
APIC 1

1 new share × $2 market = $2. Par ($1) → CS, excess ($1) → APIC.

Key insight: Both rearrange equity — R/E shrinks, paid-in capital grows. Neither changes total equity. The difference is the measurement basis: par (large) vs market (small).

Stock Splits vs Stock Dividends

Starting point: 10 shares, $1 par, $2 market. Both result in 20 shares.

2-for-1 Split 100% Stock Dividend
Shares outstanding 20 20
Par value/share $0.50 $1.00
Total par $10 $20
Retained Earnings No change −$10
APIC No change No change
Total Equity No change No change
Journal entry? None (memo only) Yes

Key insight: A split re-slices the pie — par adjusts, nothing moves between accounts. A stock dividend permanently locks R/E into legal capital. Both leave total equity unchanged.

Real World: Amazon vs Tesla

Amazon (2022)

20-for-1 stock split

  • Par value adjusted: $0.01 → $0.01 (already minimal)
  • Contributed capital: unchanged
  • No journal entry — memo only

Tesla (2022)

3-for-1 stock dividend

  • Par value: unchanged at $0.001
  • Contributed capital: increased by ~$2M
  • R/E decreased by ~$2M

Key insight: Same economic result — more shares, lower price per share. Different books. Amazon moved nothing between accounts; Tesla shifted a small amount from R/E to Common Stock.

Why Companies Buy Back Stock

  • Return capital to shareholders — alternative to dividends, often more tax-efficient
  • Signal undervaluation — management believes the stock is worth more than the market price
  • Boost EPS — fewer shares outstanding means higher earnings per share
  • Offset dilution — absorb shares created by employee stock option exercises
  • Flexibility — no implied commitment to continue, unlike dividends which markets treat as a promise

Treasury (Cost) vs Retirement — Side by Side

Example: Originally issued 10 shares at $1 par. Now repurchase 4 shares at $1.50 each ($6 total).

Cost (Treasury) Method

"We're parking these shares."

Treasury Stock 6
Cash 6

One contra-equity line at cost. Shares remain "issued" but not "outstanding."

Retirement Method

"These shares are gone forever."

Common Stock 4
Retained Earnings 2
Cash 6

Unwinds original issuance. Par ($1 × 4 = $4) removed from CS. Excess cost ($2) hits R/E.

Key insight: Same cash out ($6). Treasury defers the settlement — shares sit in the company's pocket. Retirement settles immediately — shares are cancelled. Both reduce total equity by $6. Neither touches the income statement.

Reissuing Treasury Shares

Reissue 1 treasury share (originally repurchased at $1.50).

Reissue Above Cost ($2.00)

Cash 2.00
Treasury Stock 1.50
APIC — Treasury 0.50

Excess over cost goes to APIC — Treasury Stock.

Reissue Below Cost ($1.00)

Cash 1.00
APIC — Treasury 0.50
Treasury Stock 1.50

Shortfall hits APIC — Treasury first. If exhausted, the remainder goes to R/E.

Important: Never debit a "loss" to the income statement from stock transactions. All differences go through equity accounts only (APIC or R/E).

The Golden Rule

GAAP prohibits recognizing gains or losses on the income statement from transactions in a company's own stock.

All differences between what the company pays and what it receives for its own shares go through equity accounts only — APIC or Retained Earnings. Never the income statement.

Why? Buying or selling your own stock is a capital transaction with owners, not a profit-generating activity. You can't "earn" money by trading in your own shares.

Key insight: This applies to ALL stock transactions: issuance, repurchase, reissuance, and retirement. The income statement is reserved for business operations, not capital transactions.

Retirement Method

Starbucks also uses the retirement method — buyback cost flows through Retained Earnings, creating an accumulated deficit. No Treasury Stock line.

Key insight: Negative equity does not mean insolvent — Starbucks has strong cash flows, it has simply returned more capital to shareholders than it has retained.

Apple vs Starbucks — Same Method, Different Stage

Both companies use the retirement method for share repurchases — no Treasury Stock line, buyback cost reduces paid-in capital and Retained Earnings directly.

Apple

R/E has recovered — FY2025 R/E is positive ($14.3B) despite $700B+ in cumulative buybacks (estimated). Massive earnings power replenishes retained earnings faster than buybacks deplete them.

Starbucks

R/E is still deeply negative ($8.3B deficit). Same retirement method, but Starbucks is at a different point in the capital return cycle where cumulative buybacks and dividends far exceed cumulative earnings.

Takeaway: The accounting method is the same. The difference is purely about scale of earnings relative to capital returned. Same method, different stages of the capital return cycle.
