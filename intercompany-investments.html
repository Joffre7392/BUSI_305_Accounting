Intercompany Investments

Lectures 21–22 — HTM, FV-NI, AFS, Equity Method & OCI

L 21-22

The Big Idea

More influence over the investee → more of their results show up in YOUR financial statements.

When Company A buys shares of Company B, the accounting depends on how much influence A has over B. GAAP defines three levels based on ownership percentage and other factors.

Three Levels of Influence

Passive

<20% ownership

No influence. You watch the stock price and collect dividends. Multiple methods depending on security type and intent.

Significant Influence

20–50% ownership

Board seats, JVs, strategic partnerships. One method: the Equity Method.

Control

>50% ownership

Full consolidation — out of scope for this class.

Which Method Do I Use?

Security Type Ownership Intent Method
Debt <20% Hold to maturity HTM
Debt <20% Might sell AFS
Debt <20% Trading FV through NI
Equity <20% Any FV through NI (required since 2018)
Equity 20–50% Significant influence Equity Method
Equity >50% Control Consolidation (not covered)

Key insight: For equity securities, there is no choice at <20%: FV through NI is required since ASU 2016-01 (2018). For debt, classification depends on your intent and ability.

Four Methods at a Glance

Method B/S Value Unrealized G/L Dividends Applies To
HTM Historical cost Ignored Income Debt (hold to maturity)

AFS Fair value OCI → AOCI Income Debt (might sell)
Equity Adjusted cost Tracks book value Reduce investment 20–50% equity

Important: Under the Equity Method, dividends are not income — they reduce the investment balance. This is the #1 exam trap.

Back to the Equation

Purchase: swap one asset for another (Cash ↓, Investment ↑). Total A unchanged. A = L + SE holds.

Fair value changes: under FV-NI, Investment changes with a matching R/E change (through NI). Under AFS, Investment changes with a matching AOCI change. Either way, A = L + SE.

Equity Method earnings: Investment ↑ and R/E ↑ (through equity income). Dividends: Cash ↑, Investment ↓ — asset swap, no income.

Held-to-Maturity (HTM)

You buy a bond and promise to hold it until maturity. Since you're never going to sell, market price fluctuations are irrelevant. Keep the investment at historical cost.

B/S Value

Historical cost

Unrealized G/L

Ignored completely

Income Recognition

Interest + gain/loss on sale only

Intuition: The bond will pay face value at maturity no matter what the market does in between. Temporary price swings don't affect your cash flows, so why would they affect your financial statements?

Fair Value through Net Income (FV-NI)

You own a small piece (<20%) of a company's stock, or you're actively trading debt. Every period, update to fair value. Gains and losses hit income immediately — even if unrealized.

B/S Value

Fair value (updated every period)

Unrealized G/L

Net Income — immediately

Income Recognition

Every period (volatile)

Real-world impact: After ASU 2016-01 (2018), all equity securities must use FV-NI. Berkshire Hathaway's quarterly earnings now swing billions based on stock market moves. Buffett calls it “meaningless in terms of understanding our economic performance.”

Available-for-Sale (AFS)

The middle ground. Balance sheet shows fair value (investors see real value), but unrealized gains/losses go to OCI instead of net income (income statement is protected from volatility).

B/S Value

Fair value

Unrealized G/L

OCI → AOCI (parked)

On Sale

AOCI “recycles” into NI

Key insight: AFS is only available for debt securities since 2018. Equity must go through NI. Banks pushed for this compromise because $50B bond portfolios create massive earnings volatility when rates move.

Back to the Equation

HTM: Investment stays at cost. Only interest (Cash ↑, R/E ↑) and sale (Cash ↑, Investment ↓, R/E change) affect the equation.

FV-NI: Investment adjusts to fair value each period. The offsetting entry goes to R/E (through unrealized gain/loss in NI).

AFS: Investment adjusts to fair value, but the offset goes to AOCI (in SE, but not R/E). On sale, AOCI recycles to R/E through NI.

What Is OCI?

Other Comprehensive Income is a parking lot for certain gains and losses that GAAP doesn't want in net income yet.

Net Income → Retained Earnings

NI = this period's earnings.
R/E = running total of all NI.

OCI → AOCI

OCI = this period's parked G/L.
AOCI = running total of all OCI.

Key insight: AOCI accumulates OCI over time, just like R/E accumulates NI. OCI is a flow (this period); AOCI is a stock (cumulative). Both live in Stockholders' Equity.

The Parking Lot Metaphor

Under AFS, unrealized gains and losses are “parked” in AOCI. They sit there, accumulating, until you sell the investment.

Unrealized G/L

→ FV-NI: straight to NI

Unrealized G/L

→

OCI → AOCI

→ on sale: recycle to NI

How Recycling Works

When you sell an AFS security, the “parking lot” empties. Three things happen simultaneously:

1

AOCI zeroes out. Whatever accumulated in AOCI gets reversed with an offsetting OCI entry.

2

Real gain/loss goes to NI. Sale price minus original cost = the real economic gain or loss.

3

Investment removed from B/S. Carrying value (fair value) is derecognized.

Why recycle? If we left gains in AOCI forever, net income would never reflect the real economic result. Over the full life, NI must equal the true gain. Recycling ensures that.

AFS: Debt Securities Only (Since 2018)

Equity Securities

Must go through NI. No exceptions. No sheltering in OCI. This is what Buffett was upset about.

Debt Securities

Your choice: HTM (cost), AFS (FV through OCI), or Trading (FV through NI).

=
Back to the Equation

Under AFS, unrealized gains increase both Assets (Investment ↑) and SE (AOCI ↑). On sale, AOCI resets to zero and R/E captures the real gain through NI. Total SE stays the same; it just shifts from AOCI to R/E. A = L + SE always holds.

A Fundamentally Different Relationship

Passive (All Prior Methods)

You watched market prices, collected dividends, and reported gains or losses. You had no say in how the company was run.

Equity Method (20–50%)

You might have a board seat. You participate in strategy. This isn't a stock in your portfolio — it's a relationship. GAAP says: stop tracking market price. Track your share of what the company actually earns.

The Core Idea

Your investment balance tracks your share of the investee's book value.

When they earn money

Your investment goes up by your % × their NI. You record equity income.

When they pay dividends

Your investment goes down. Cash left the company. This is NOT income.

One-line consolidation: Instead of combining every asset and liability, collapse the investee into one B/S line (Investment) and one I/S line (Equity Income).

Why Aren't Dividends Income?

Passive Investor

Company sends me $2. That's income! I have no other way to see their earnings. I just watch the stock price and cash dividend checks.

Equity Method Investor

I already booked $4 when S earned it. This $2 is not new. S is converting retained earnings into cash. My investment shrinks because S's equity shrinks. No new income.

Exam trap: In every passive method, dividends = income. Under the equity method, dividends = return OF capital, not return ON capital. Double-counting is the danger.

The Investment T-Account

The T-account tells the entire story of your relationship with the investee:

Investment in Company S

Debit (Increases)

Cost $100

Share of NI $4

(40% × $10)

Credit (Decreases)

Dividends $2

Removed on sale $102

Balance: $102

Cost + Share of Earnings − Dividends = Balance. The balance tracks your share of S's book value.

=
Back to the Equation

S earns $10: Investment ↑ $4, R/E ↑ $4 (through equity income). Assets and SE both rise.

S pays dividend: Cash ↑ $2, Investment ↓ $2. Asset swap — total A unchanged, SE unchanged. No income.

Sale at $110: Cash ↑ $110, Investment ↓ $102. Gain of $8 goes to R/E through NI.
