Lecture 18 — Operating vs. finance leases & their financial statement effects

L 18

What Is a Lease?

"A lease is a contract that gives the lessee the right to use an identified asset for a period of time in exchange for periodic payments."

Lease

  • Pay to use the asset
  • Owner retains legal title
  • Fixed term and fixed payments

Purchase

  • Pay to own the asset
  • Title transfers to you
  • One-time cost (or financed)

Key insight: Think renting an apartment vs. buying a house — same roof over your head, very different economics.

Leases Are Everywhere

$3T+

in lease obligations on US balance sheets

80%+

of companies use operating leases

Airlines

lease most of their fleet

Common leased assets:

  • Office space
  • Retail stores
  • Warehouses
  • Airplanes
  • Delivery trucks
  • Heavy equipment
  • IT equipment
  • Copiers/printers
  • Medical devices

The Accounting Question

Before 2019, most leases were off-balance-sheet — invisible to investors. ASC 842 changed that: all leases now appear on the balance sheet.

Key insight: Two types remain: Finance Lease (more like buying) vs. Operating Lease (more like renting).

The Five Tests (ASC 842)

A lease is a finance lease if it meets any one of these criteria:

# Test What It Means
1 Ownership transfer Title passes to lessee at end of lease
2 Purchase option Lessee has option to buy at bargain price
3 Lease term ≥ 75% of useful life Lessee uses asset for most of its life
4 PV of payments ≥ 90% of fair value Lessee pays for nearly all of the asset's value
5 Specialized asset Asset has no alternative use to lessor

Key insight: If the lessee gets substantially all the benefits and bears substantially all the risks — it's a purchase in disguise.

Meets ANY test

→ Finance Lease

Meets NONE

→ Operating Lease

Key insight: Both types go on the balance sheet as an ROU Asset and Lease Liability. The difference is how expense hits the income statement.

Initial Recognition (Both Types)

ROU Asset = Lease Liability = PV(Lease Payments)

Dr Right-of-Use Asset (+A) PV of payments
Cr Lease Liability (+L) PV of payments
=
Back to the Equation

When a lease is signed, a new asset (ROU Asset) and a new liability (Lease Liability) appear on the balance sheet for the same amount. A increases, L increases by the same amount — A = L + SE still holds. No expense is recognized on Day 1.

Finance Lease Example Setup

A company leases a delivery van for 3 years. Payments of $10,000 due at year-end. Discount rate: 3%. Useful life: 3 years, reverts to lessor.

Finance lease — lease term = 100% of useful life (Test 3).

PV = 10,000 × (1 − 1.03−3) / 0.03 = 10,000 × 2.8286

Key insight: Lease Liability = ROU Asset = $28,286

Day 1 Journal Entry

Dr Right-of-Use Asset 28,286
Cr Lease Liability 28,286

Balance Sheet Effect

  • Assets +28,286
  • Liabilities +28,286
  • Equity unchanged

Key Point

No cash changes hands yet.

Lease Liability Amortization Logic

Opening × 3%

= Interest

Payment − Interest

= Principal

Opening − Principal

= Closing

Two Expenses Each Period

Interest Expense

From lease liability

  • Effective interest method
  • Decreases over time
  • Y1: $849 → Y3: $291

Amortization Expense

From ROU asset

  • Straight-line over lease term
  • Constant each period
  • $28,286 ÷ 3 = $9,429/yr

Key insight: Front-loaded: Year 1 = $10,278 vs. Year 3 = $9,719 — just like a mortgage.

Year 1 Journal Entries

Lease Payment (Year 1):

Dr Interest Expense 849
Dr Lease Liability 9,151
Cr Cash 10,000

ROU Amortization (Year 1):

Dr Amortization Expense 9,429
Cr Accum Amort — ROU 9,429

Total Year 1 Expense: Interest $849 + Amortization $9,429 = $10,278

=
Back to the Equation

Each year has two effects: (1) the lease payment splits into Interest Expense (reducing SE) and principal (reducing L), funded by Cash (reducing A). (2) Amortization reduces the ROU Asset (A decreases) and SE (expense). Over time, both the asset and liability wind down to zero.

Operating Lease Example

Same delivery van. Same $10,000 annual payments. Same 3% rate. Same 3-year term. But now the van has a useful life of 6 years (lease term = 50% of useful life). None of the five tests are met → operating lease.

Initial Recognition — Same as Finance

Dr Right-of-Use Asset 28,286
Cr Lease Liability 28,286

Key insight: Day 1 is identical. The difference is entirely about how expense is recognized going forward.

Straight-Line Expense

Annual Lease Expense = Total Payments / Lease Term = $30,000 / 3 = $10,000/year

Income Statement

  • Single line — Lease Expense
  • $10,000/yr
  • Flat and simple

Balance Sheet

  • ROU Asset and Lease Liability both decline
  • They stay equal

Key insight: Operating lease expense works like rent: same amount, same line, every period.

Year 1 Journal Entry (Simplified)

Dr Lease Expense 10,000
Cr Cash 10,000

Behind the scenes:

Lease Liability ROU Asset
Start $28,286 $28,286
After Y1 $19,135 $19,135
After Y2 $9,709 $9,709

Technical note: Interest accrues on liability; ROU amortizes by a "plug" — but you record one entry.

Key insight: Same total expense ($30,000). Same cash paid ($30,000). The only difference is the timing pattern on the income statement.

Back to the Equation

Each operating lease payment is a single expense that reduces Cash (A decreases) and Retained Earnings via Lease Expense (SE decreases). The ROU Asset and Lease Liability decline by equal amounts each period, keeping A = L + SE in balance.

Key Differences

Finance Lease

  • Two I/S lines (interest + amortization)
  • Front-loaded expense
  • Interest appears below operating income

Operating Lease

  • One I/S line (lease expense)
  • Flat expense pattern
  • All expense is "operating"

Lessee Accounting Summary

Finance Lease

B/S: ROU asset + Lease liability

I/S: Amortization expense (straight-line) + Interest expense (effective interest)

Cash Flows: Interest → operating, Principal → financing

Operating Lease

B/S: ROU asset + Lease liability

I/S: Single lease expense (straight-line)

Cash Flows: Entire lease payment → operating

Expense Pattern

=
Back to the Equation

Both lease types produce the same total expense ($30,000) and same total cash outflow ($30,000). The difference is timing: finance leases recognize more expense early, operating leases spread it evenly. Analysts adjust for this when comparing companies.

Example 1: Finance Lease Full Lifecycle

A company leases equipment for 4 years. Annual payments of $15,000 at year-end. Rate: 5%. Useful life: 4 years (Test 3 met → finance lease).

Step 1: Compute the present value of lease payments

PV = 15,000 × (1 − 1.05−4) / 0.05

1.05−4 = 0.82270

Factor = (1 − 0.82270) / 0.05 = 3.5460

PV = 15,000 × 3.5460 = $53,189

Step 2: Record Day 1 journal entry
Right-of-Use Asset53,189
Lease Liability53,189

Both the ROU Asset and Lease Liability begin at the present value of lease payments.

Step 3: Build Year 1 amortization row
YearOpeningPaymentInterestPrincipalClosing
1$53,189$15,000$2,659$12,341$40,848

Interest = $53,189 × 5% = $2,659. Principal = $15,000 − $2,659 = $12,341. Closing = $53,189 − $12,341 = $40,848.

Step 4: Record Year 1 journal entries

Payment entry:

Interest Expense2,659
Lease Liability12,341
Cash15,000

Amortization entry:

Amortization Expense13,297
Accumulated Amort — ROU Asset13,297

ROU amortization = $53,189 / 4 = $13,297 per year (straight-line).

Total Year 1 expense = $2,659 (interest) + $13,297 (amortization) = $15,956

Example 2: Operating Lease

Same equipment, same terms. But useful life is 10 years (lease term = 40% of useful life). No tests met → operating lease.

Step 1: Compute the present value (same calculation)

PV = 15,000 × (1 − 1.05−4) / 0.05 = 15,000 × 3.5460

PV = $53,189

The PV calculation is identical regardless of lease classification.

Step 2: Record Day 1 journal entry (same)
Right-of-Use Asset53,189
Lease Liability53,189

Day 1 entry is identical for both lease types under ASC 842.

Step 3: Record Year 1 operating lease entry

Lease Expense = Total payments / Lease term = $60,000 / 4 = $15,000 per year

Lease Expense15,000
Cash15,000

Operating leases recognize a single, straight-line lease expense each period. No separate interest or amortization line items.
