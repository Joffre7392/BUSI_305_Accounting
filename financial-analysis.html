Lecture 25 — The 7 metrics that actually matter & where to dig deeper

L 25
The Thesis

Every ratio is a tripwire, not an answer.

A ratio tells you where to look next. The real work happens in footnotes, MD&A, non-GAAP reconciliations, and peer comp sets. This unit teaches you to stop treating ratios as conclusions and start treating them as questions. The 7 metrics below are the ones that actually matter — ranked by how often they drive real decisions.

The 7 Metrics That Actually Matter

Ranked by real-world importance — how often each ratio drives a buy/sell/credit/M&A decision in practice.

Three Questions to Carry Forward

Every time you look at a ratio, ask these three questions before you form an opinion:

1. What's the numerator — really?

Is this GAAP or adjusted? Trailing or forward? Whose estimate? A single ratio name can hide five different calculations.

2. Is the trend consistent with the story?

A ratio in isolation is noise. A trend is the signal. Does margin compression line up with stated guidance? Does ROIC track capital-allocation claims?

3. How does it compare to close peers?

“Gross margin of 35%” is meaningless alone. vs. industry median of 45%? A 10-point gap. vs. 28%? Pricing power.

=
Back to the Equation

Every ratio on this page is a ratio of pieces of A = L + SE and the flows that move them. Margins are built from the income statement (flows into SE). Leverage compares L to a flow. ROIC takes a slice of SE plus L (invested capital) and compares it to an IS flow (NOPAT). The equation holds underneath every one of them.

The Four EPS's Students Must Distinguish

“EPS” is four different numbers depending on who's asking. Confusing them is the single biggest rookie error in earnings analysis.

1

GAAP Basic EPS

(NI − Preferred Div) / Wtd-Avg Basic Shares

Legally mandated. Useful mainly as an input to the dilution calculation — almost no one values on this directly.

2

GAAP Diluted EPS

(NI − Pref Div) / Diluted Shares

The honest GAAP number. Adds RSUs, options (treasury-stock method), and convertibles (if-converted). This is what shows up in filings.

3

Non-GAAP / Adjusted EPS

GAAP EPS + company-defined add-backs

Company-defined. Typically adds back SBC, intangible amortization from acquisitions, restructuring, and “one-time” items. Management picks the list.

4

Street / Consensus EPS

Mean / median of sell-side estimates

Almost always non-GAAP. FactSet vs. Refinitiv vs. Bloomberg can give different numbers. The “beat/miss” depends on which consensus you use.

The trap: A headline like “Company beats EPS by $0.05” is comparing company non-GAAP to sell-side consensus non-GAAP. The GAAP number could have missed. Always ask: which EPS?

What Analysts Actually Do (Earnings Day)

The press release hits. You have 10 minutes before the stock moves. Here's the order of operations.

1.

Headline EPS vs. consensus

Beat, miss, or in-line? First read — then immediately stop trusting it.

2.

Revenue vs. consensus

EPS can be engineered through buybacks, tax, and SBC. Revenue is much harder to fake. A revenue miss with an EPS beat is a red flag, not a win.

3.

Forward guidance

Often moves the stock more than the print. “Beat and raise” vs. “beat and lower” are completely different outcomes.

4.

Quality of the beat

Revenue-driven (good). Margin-driven (good). Opex discipline (mixed). Tax rate (bad — rate can't keep dropping). Buybacks (cosmetic). New non-GAAP add-backs (suspect).

Within the first hour

5.

Non-GAAP reconciliation table

The real story. Compare this quarter's add-backs line by line to last quarter. Did new items appear? Did “one-time” restructuring show up for the 6th consecutive quarter?

6.

Share count trend

Down = buybacks supporting EPS. Flat despite buybacks = SBC issuance canceling the repurchases — so employees, not shareholders, got the benefit.

7.

Effective tax rate

A beat driven by tax dropping from 24% to 18% isn't a real beat. The rate can't keep falling forever.

The P/E Family — Pick Your E

Ratio Formula When It's Useful
Trailing P/E Price / EPS (last 4 Q) Historical, clean, backward-looking. Uncontroversial but stale.
Forward P/E Price / Consensus EPS (next 4 Q) What markets actually use. But sensitive to GAAP vs. non-GAAP choice.
PEG (Forward P/E) / (Growth rate %) Normalizes P/E for growth. Rough heuristic — sub-1.0 is “cheap for the growth.”
EV/EBITDA (Market Cap + Net Debt) / EBITDA Capital-structure neutral. Comparing across companies with different leverage.
P/S Price / Sales/share When earnings don't exist — early-stage, cyclical trough, or pre-profitability tech.
P/B Price / Book Value/share Legacy metric. Still dominant for banks and insurers, irrelevant for tech.

Practitioner punchline: Nobody valuable looks at P/E without first asking “on what E?” The E you pick changes the answer more than the P.

EPS Red Flags

Non-GAAP growing while GAAP stagnates

The add-back list is doing the work. Management is paid on non-GAAP; the widening gap is the warning.

Add-back list growing or adding novel items

If “CEO transition costs” became a new line this quarter, someone decided it should be excluded from how you measure them.

“One-time” items that appear quarterly

Six consecutive quarters of restructuring costs means restructuring is the business.

Share count flat or rising despite buybacks

Gross buybacks + SBC = what really happened. Watch net dilution.

Abnormally low or volatile tax rate

18% “effective rate” on a 21% statutory rate needs a footnote explanation.

EPS beats, revenue misses — repeatedly

Demand is softening; cost cuts and tax tricks are holding up the number until they can't.

Where to Dig Deeper

When an EPS number raises a question, these are the primary sources:

  • →EPS footnote in 10-K/10-Q — full reconciliation of basic vs. diluted shares, list of antidilutive securities, detail on convertibles.
  • →Non-GAAP reconciliation table — usually at the end of the earnings press release. Read every line. For each: would I agree this is non-recurring?
  • →Share repurchase disclosure + SBC disclosure — net dilution = issuance − repurchases. Negative number = shareholders got something back.
  • →Cash flow statement, financing section — actual dollars spent on buybacks, versus dollars announced in buyback press releases.
  • →Proxy (DEF 14A) — if non-GAAP EPS is the comp metric, management has an incentive to expand the add-back list. Check the compensation discussion.

Free Cash Flow — The Serious Investor's Metric

Earnings are an opinion; cash is a fact. FCF is what's left after the business has reinvested enough to stay alive. It's the ceiling on everything: dividends, buybacks, debt reduction, M&A.

Textbook formula

FCF = CFO − CapEx

FCF Yield

FCF / Market Cap

Equivalently: FCF-per-share / Price

FCF Yield — rules of thumb

>8% Cheap or broken — dig in
5–8% Worth a look
2–5% Priced for growth
<2% Priced for heroic growth

Where FCF Is Used in Practice

  • Every PE / LBO model — the DCF is built on FCF, not net income
  • Buffett's “owner earnings” — conceptually FCF with a capex tweak
  • Credit analysis — bondholders get paid from cash, not earnings
  • The ceiling on capital return (dividends + buybacks)

FCF Red Flags

  • CFO < Net Income persistently (earnings aren't converting to cash)
  • “Adjusted FCF” that adds SBC back — SBC is compensation, just paid in stock
  • CapEx running well below D&A for years — the business is under-investing
  • Working capital a persistent tailwind — stretching suppliers can't last forever

ROIC — The Quality Metric

ROIC asks: is the underlying business good at turning capital into profit — independent of how it's financed? A high-ROIC business with modest leverage beats a mediocre business juiced with debt. It's the metric the best long-term investors (Terry Smith, Nick Sleep, Chuck Akre) watch above all others.

Formula

ROIC = NOPAT / Invested Capital

NOPAT = Operating Income × (1 − tax rate)

Invested Capital = Equity + Debt − Cash (roughly)

The benchmark: ROIC − WACC. A business that consistently earns above its cost of capital creates value. One that earns below destroys it, no matter how fast it grows.

ROIC — rules of thumb

<10% Mediocre business
10–15% Average
15–25% Good
25%+ Exceptional

Where to Dig

  • Definition variants: with or without goodwill, after-tax or pretax, invested capital measured how? Compare companies using the same definition.
  • Segment ROIC: Consolidated ROIC can hide wildly divergent businesses (e.g., AWS vs. Amazon retail).
  • Persistence: A single year of high ROIC is noise. 10 years of 20%+ is a moat.
  • ROIC vs. WACC spread: The spread, not the level, is what creates shareholder value per dollar reinvested.

ROIC Red Flags

  • ROIC declining while ROE is rising — the business got worse, but leverage is hiding it
  • ROIC below WACC but management still reinvesting heavily — value destruction at scale
  • Big gap between ROIC-including-goodwill and ROIC-excluding-goodwill — acquisitions aren't earning their purchase price

ROIC vs. ROE — The Leverage Question

ROE (Return on Equity) is the older, simpler cousin of ROIC. But it's polluted by leverage: a company that borrows a lot and buys back shares can lift ROE without the business changing at all. ROIC strips this away.

When ROE rises faster than ROIC

The gap is leverage or buybacks — financial engineering. The underlying business improved, but shareholders are getting most of the benefit from a smaller equity base, not a better business.

Directional read: Better, but less better than ROE suggests.

When ROIC rises and ROE falls

Rare, but tells a clean story: the business got better (ROIC up) while the company de-levered or issued equity (ROE down from denominator growth). Watch for balance sheet repair.

Directional read: Unambiguously better business.

Rule: When ROE and ROIC diverge, trust ROIC for the business question. ROE tells you about ownership structure, which is a different question.

=
Back to the Equation

ROIC's denominator is invested capital = Debt + Equity − Cash, i.e., the L-and-SE side of A = L + SE minus the most liquid piece of A. ROIC essentially asks: given the financing the company actually uses, how well is that capital working? FCF, meanwhile, is the cash-basis analogue of net income — it feeds back into cash on the A side.

Margins — Where Value Is Made or Lost

Each margin strips off a different layer of the income statement. The trend and the level relative to peers matter more than any absolute number.

Gross Margin

(Revenue − COGS) / Revenue

Pricing power & unit economics. The earliest warning indicator — if GM compresses, something upstream has changed (input costs, pricing, mix).

Operating Margin

Operating Income / Revenue

Operating leverage. When revenue grows faster than opex, operating margin expands. When it doesn't, the model is broken.

Net Margin

Net Income / Revenue

Post-everything. Affected by tax & interest expense. Least useful for comparing businesses; most useful for comparing earnings power across capital structures.

What to dig into

  • Segment margins — consolidated can hide divergence. Amazon's AWS and retail have totally different margin profiles.
  • YoY change, not absolute level — a 45% GM is meaningless; a 45% GM that was 50% a year ago is a story.
  • Gross margin decomposition: volume / price / mix / input costs — which one moved?

Red flag: Net margin rising while operating margin falls — tax engineering, not business improvement. Also: margin expansion entirely from opex cuts (can't keep cutting forever).

Leverage — Financial Risk on Top of Business Risk

Workhorse ratio

Net Debt / EBITDA

Net Debt = Total Debt − Cash

Used as a covenant standard in credit, as a sizing metric in LBOs, and as an M&A-capacity gauge for strategic buyers. A stable 2x levered company isn't the same animal as a 6x levered company, even if the income statements look similar.

Net Debt / EBITDA — rules of thumb

<2× Conservative
2–4× Typical corporate
4–6× Levered (LBO-like)
>6× Distressed territory

Where to dig

  • Off-balance-sheet items: pensions, legal reserves, factored receivables, purchase commitments — these behave like debt
  • Debt maturity schedule — a maturity wall is a refinancing risk, not a leverage problem
  • Fixed vs. floating rate mix — matters enormously when rates move
  • Covenant headroom & interest coverage (EBITDA / Interest) — 3x+ is comfortable, 1.5x is a problem

Red flags: Leverage rising while EBITDA is flat (debt-funded growth that isn't growing). EBITDA is “adjusted” (same non-GAAP trick as EPS, but on the leverage side). Buybacks funded by new debt. Near-term maturity wall without committed refinancing.

Liquidity — Matters When It Matters

For a healthy big-cap, liquidity ratios are mostly dormant — the company has ample cash and a revolving credit line. They become urgent for cyclicals at the bottom of the cycle, for small-caps without credit facilities, and for any company heading into distress. Don't ignore them, but don't over-weight them either.

Ratio Formula What it tells you
Current Ratio Current Assets / Current Liabilities Broadest measure. >1 = current assets cover short-term obligations.
Quick Ratio (Cash + ST Invest + A/R) / CL Strips out inventory. Useful when inventory is slow-moving or illiquid.
Cash Ratio (Cash + ST Invest) / CL Strictest. Can the company pay its bills today, without touching inventory or A/R?

Red flags: Current ratio below 1.0 combined with rising DSO (customers paying slower). Quick ratio much less than current ratio (inventory is propping up liquidity). Cash ratio falling while current assets rise (the quality of current assets is deteriorating).

SBC — Compensation Paid in Dilution

Stock-based compensation is real compensation. The company isn't paying cash — it's paying by handing over a piece of the company. Calling SBC “non-cash” misses the point: the cost shows up in dilution, not cash outflow. This is why “adjusted FCF” that excludes SBC is a red herring.

SBC / Revenue — tech screen

<5%Restrained
5–15%Typical tech
15–25%Yellow flag
>25%Red flag

SBC / FCF — the sharper cut

If SBC is a large % of FCF, it means a large fraction of the company's distributable cash is really going to employees as equity compensation. The “remaining” cash available for shareholders is smaller than it appears.

SBC/FCF > 50% in “profitable” tech companies is surprisingly common.

Red flags: SBC growing faster than revenue (comp inflation without leverage). Share count rising despite announced buybacks (buybacks just offsetting issuance — shareholders get nothing). SBC exceeds GAAP net income (the whole profit is being “paid” in dilution).

The Skeptic's Lens — Signs Something's Off

A consolidated checklist you can apply to any company. Not every item is disqualifying — each is a question that deserves an answer before you form a view.

Earnings quality

  • Non-GAAP EPS rising while GAAP EPS is flat or declining
  • “One-time” restructuring costs that appear every quarter
  • Add-back list growing or adding novel items
  • EPS beat driven by tax rate, not operations
  • Share count flat despite reported buybacks (SBC is the story)

Cash quality

  • CFO persistently below net income
  • “Adjusted FCF” that adds SBC back to CFO
  • CapEx running well below D&A for multiple years
  • Working capital a recurring tailwind (stretching payables)

Business quality

  • Gross margin declining for multiple consecutive quarters
  • Net margin up while operating margin down (tax-engineering gain)
  • ROIC falling while ROE rising (leverage masking deterioration)
  • ROIC below WACC with continued heavy reinvestment

Capital structure

  • Leverage rising while EBITDA is flat
  • EBITDA reported on an “adjusted” basis
  • Buybacks funded by new debt (not retained cash)
  • Near-term debt maturity wall without refinancing in place
  • Current ratio <1 with rising DSO

Ownership & incentives

  • SBC >20% of revenue (and growing)
  • SBC exceeds net income
  • Non-GAAP EPS is the management compensation metric (check the proxy)
  • Insider selling heavy; insider buying absent

Back to the Equation

Margins decompose the income statement flow into SE. Leverage compares the L side to an IS flow. Liquidity compares current pieces of A to current pieces of L. SBC moves stock (issued from SE) to employees in exchange for services (expense against SE). The same A = L + SE is rearranging under every ratio on this page.

Example 1: Non-GAAP EPS Bridge

MegaSoft Inc. reports the following results for FY25 (stylized teaching data, in millions of dollars except EPS):

GAAP Net Income$80,000
Diluted weighted-avg shares8,000
GAAP Diluted EPS$10.00

Company-provided non-GAAP reconciliation (pretax add-backs):

+ Stock-based compensation$7,000
+ Intangible amortization (from acquisitions)2,000
+ “One-time” restructuring1,000
Total pretax add-backs$10,000
− Tax on add-backs (@ 20%)(2,000)
After-tax add-backs$8,000
Solution: Compute non-GAAP EPS and interpret the beat.

Step 1: Bridge GAAP NI to non-GAAP NI

Non-GAAP NI = GAAP NI + After-tax add-backs

= $80,000 + $8,000 = $88,000

Step 2: Divide by diluted shares

Non-GAAP EPS = $88,000 / 8,000 = $11.00

Step 3: Interpret

If sell-side consensus was $10.80, the company “beat” by $0.20. But look at what drove the $1.00 gap between GAAP ($10.00) and non-GAAP ($11.00):

  • $0.70 from SBC — real compensation paid in dilution. Not a “one-time” anything.
  • $0.20 from intangible amortization — defensible if acquisitions are truly finished; suspect if the company keeps acquiring.
  • $0.10 from restructuring — defensible only if this is genuinely non-recurring. Check prior quarters.

The skeptic's read: the beat is mostly cosmetic. GAAP growth is the number that deserves real attention.

Example 2: FCF & FCF Yield

Same company, FY25 cash flow summary (in millions except per-share and price data):

Cash from Operations (CFO)$120,000
− Capital Expenditures(50,000)
Diluted shares8,000
Share price$350.00
Solution: Compute FCF, FCF/share, FCF yield, then interpret.

Step 1: FCF

FCF = CFO − CapEx = $120,000 − $50,000 = $70,000

Step 2: FCF per share

FCF / share = $70,000 / 8,000 = $8.75

Step 3: FCF yield

FCF Yield = FCF / share ÷ Price = $8.75 / $350.00 = 2.50%

Equivalently: Market cap = $350 × 8,000 = $2,800,000M. FCF / Market Cap = $70,000 / $2,800,000 = 2.50%.

Interpretation

A 2.5% FCF yield falls in the “priced for growth” band (2–5%). The market is paying ~40 times current FCF, which is fine if FCF grows rapidly from here and risky if it doesn't. Compare with a 3-year Treasury — the equity is offering less current yield than risk-free debt. All the return has to come from growth.

Example 3: ROIC vs. ROE Divergence

The same company reports these two-year trends (in millions):

Metric Year 1 Year 2
NOPAT$2,000$2,400
Invested Capital$10,000$11,000
Net Income$1,600$2,000
Avg. Stockholders' Equity$8,000$7,000

Between Year 1 and Year 2, the company bought back ~$1,000M of stock and added $1,000M of debt to fund it.

Solution: Compute ROIC and ROE for both years, then decide what each tells you.

ROIC

Year 1: 2,000 / 10,000 = 20.0%

Year 2: 2,400 / 11,000 = 21.8%

Δ = +180 bp

ROE

Year 1: 1,600 / 8,000 = 20.0%

Year 2: 2,000 / 7,000 = 28.6%

Δ = +860 bp

What it means

Both metrics are up. ROIC is up ~180 basis points — the underlying business has gotten modestly more productive. ROE is up ~860 basis points, most of which comes from a smaller equity base (buybacks) funded by new debt.

Directional read for the exam: Business getting better (ROIC up). But if you look only at ROE, you'd overstate the improvement by ~5×. The extra ROE came from financial engineering, not operational quality.

Example 4: Quality of the Beat

A Q4 earnings release from “Acme Software” (stylized teaching data):

Reported

Non-GAAP EPS$2.10
Revenue$49.0B
Effective tax rate18%

Consensus

Non-GAAP EPS$2.00
Revenue$50.0B
Expected tax rate24%
Solution: Strip out the tax effect. Is this still a beat?

Step 1: Back out pretax EPS

Pretax EPS = $2.10 ÷ (1 − 0.18) = $2.10 / 0.82 = $2.56

Step 2: Re-apply the consensus tax rate

Tax-normalized EPS = $2.56 × (1 − 0.24) = $2.56 × 0.76 = $1.95

What the headline hid

  • Revenue missed by $1.0B (2%) — demand was weaker than expected
  • The $0.10 EPS beat evaporates at consensus tax rate: tax-normalized EPS would have been $1.95, a $0.05 miss
  • The entire beat came from a lower tax rate, not operations
  • Effective rate dropped from 24% to 18% — you can't keep doing this forever

The skeptic's read: This is a low-quality beat. The business lost on the top line; the EPS line was rescued by a one-time tax benefit. Expect the stock to trade down on the print despite the “beat.”
