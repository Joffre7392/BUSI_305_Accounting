Lectures 14-16

Why $1 Today Is Worth More Than $1 Tomorrow

This is the single most important idea in finance. A dollar today is worth more than a dollar in the future for three reasons:

Earning Potential

Money received today can be invested and earn a return. A dollar today grows into more than a dollar tomorrow.

Inflation

Prices generally rise over time. A dollar buys less in the future than it does today — purchasing power erodes.

Risk

A dollar in hand is certain. A promised future dollar carries the risk that the promise may not be kept.

Simple Interest

Interest = PV × r × n

Interest is earned only on the original principal. No interest-on-interest.

Example: $1,000 at 10% for 3 years

Year 1: $100  |  Year 2: $100  |  Year 3: $100

Total = $1,000 + $300 = $1,300

Compound Interest

FV = PV × (1 + r)n

Interest is earned on the principal plus all previously earned interest. The snowball effect.

Example: $1,000 at 10% for 3 years

Year 1: $100  |  Year 2: $110  |  Year 3: $121

Total = $1,000 × 1.103 = $1,331

In A = L + SE, present value determines how long-term assets and liabilities appear on the balance sheet. A note receivable (asset) and a note payable (liability) are both reported at their present value — the discounted value of future cash flows. The discount rate reflects the time value of money and risk.

What Is an Annuity?

An annuity is a series of equal payments made at equal intervals. Two types:

PV of an Ordinary Annuity

PV = PMT × [ 1 − (1+r)−n ] ÷ r

Example: $10,000/year for 5 years at 12%

PV = $10,000 × [1 − (1.12)−5] ÷ 0.12

PV = $10,000 × [1 − 0.56743] ÷ 0.12 = $10,000 × 3.60478

PV = $36,047.76

PV of an Annuity Due

PVdue = PVordinary × (1 + r)

Since each payment arrives one period earlier, every payment is discounted one less period. The PV is simply the ordinary annuity PV multiplied by (1+r).

Key insight: An annuity due is always worth more than an ordinary annuity with the same PMT, r, and n. Money received sooner is worth more.

Perpetuities

PV = PMT ÷ r

A perpetuity is an annuity that lasts forever. The formula is beautifully simple because as n → ∞, the annuity factor converges to 1/r.

Example: An endowment pays $2,000/year forever. Rate = 14%.

PV = $2,000 ÷ 0.14 = $14,285.71

Elegance: To fund a $25,000/year scholarship at 5%, you need $25,000 ÷ 0.05 = $500,000. The principal is never touched — only the interest is paid out each year.

FV of an Annuity (Savings Plans)

FV = PMT × [ (1+r)n − 1 ] ÷ r

How much will a stream of regular deposits grow to? This is the core of retirement planning.

Retirement: $500/month at 9%/year for 40 years

r = 0.09/12 = 0.0075, n = 480

FV = $500 × [(1.0075)480 − 1] / 0.0075

FV = $2,340,660

Total deposited: $240,000. Interest earned: $2,100,660!

ETF savings: $200/month at 7%/year for 30 years

r = 0.07/12 = 0.005833, n = 360

FV = $200 × [(1.005833)360 − 1] / 0.005833

FV = $243,994

Total deposited: $72,000. Interest earned: $171,994!

Bond coupons are ordinary annuities — the PV of the coupon stream is part of the bond’s price (a liability on the issuer’s balance sheet). Lease payments are annuities due — the PV of lease payments determines the Right-of-Use Asset (A) and Lease Liability (L) on the balance sheet. In both cases, annuity formulas determine how these items enter A = L + SE.

Net Present Value (NPV)

NPV = −Initial Investment + PV of Future Cash Flows

NPV measures whether an investment creates or destroys value. It’s the single most important capital budgeting tool.

NPV > 0

Accept

Creates value

NPV = 0

Indifferent

Earns exactly the required return

NPV < 0

Reject

Destroys value

Factory Example: Invest $1,100,000 today. Receive $350,000/year for 5 years. Cost of capital = 12%.

PV of inflows = $350,000 × [1 − (1.12)−5] / 0.12 = $350,000 × 3.60478 = $1,261,673

NPV = −$1,100,000 + $1,261,673 = +$161,673

NPV > 0 → Accept the project. It creates $161,673 of value.

Internal Rate of Return (IRR)

IRR = the rate that makes NPV = 0

The IRR is the project’s own “rate of return.” Compare it to the cost of capital to decide:

IRR > Cost of Capital → Accept

Project earns more than required

IRR < Cost of Capital → Reject

Project earns less than required

Same factory: −$1,100,000 + $350,000/yr for 5 years

IRR = 18.03% > 12% cost of capital → Accept

NPV vs. IRR: When They Disagree

For a single project, NPV and IRR always give the same accept/reject answer. But for mutually exclusive projects (pick only one), they can rank differently.

Rule: When NPV and IRR conflict, always use NPV as the tiebreaker. NPV measures the actual dollar value created, while IRR can be misleading with different project sizes or timing patterns.

Back to the Equation

NPV determines whether acquiring assets creates value for stockholders’ equity. A positive-NPV project increases the value of assets by more than the cost, which flows through to SE. A negative-NPV project would decrease SE — the company is better off not investing. This is how A = L + SE connects to capital budgeting.

Step-by-Step Solution

Sensitivity Analysis

Example 1: College Savings Fund

Setup

You invest $5,000 today in a college savings account earning 7% per year, compounded annually. How much will be in the account after 18 years?

Step 1: Identify Variables

PV = $5,000  |  r = 7% = 0.07  |  n = 18 years

Step 2: Apply the Formula

FV = PV × (1 + r)n

FV = $5,000 × (1.07)18 = $5,000 × 3.37993 = $16,899.66

Example 2: House Down Payment

Setup

You want to have $80,000 for a down payment in 5 years. If you can earn 6% annually, how much do you need to invest today?

Step 1: Identify Variables

FV = $80,000  |  r = 6% = 0.06  |  n = 5 years

Step 2: Apply the Formula

PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)n

PV = $80,000 ÷ (1.06)5 = $80,000 ÷ 1.33823 = $59,780.65

Example 3: Quarterly Compounding

Setup

Invest $10,000 at 8% compounded quarterly for 5 years. What is the future value?

Step 1: Identify Variables

PV = $10,000  |  r = 8%  |  m = 4 (quarterly)  |  n = 5 years

Rate per period = 8%/4 = 2%  |  Total periods = 5 × 4 = 20

Step 2: Apply the Formula

FV = PV × (1 + r/m)n×m

FV = $10,000 × (1.02)20 = $10,000 × 1.48595 = $14,859.47

Example 4: Endowment Valuation (Perpetuity)

Setup

A university wants to fund a $25,000/year scholarship forever. The endowment earns 5% annually. How much must be donated?

Step 1: Identify as a Perpetuity

PMT = $25,000/year forever  |  r = 5% = 0.05

This is a perpetuity: PV = PMT ÷ r

Step 2: Calculate

PV = $25,000 ÷ 0.05 = $500,000

A $500,000 endowment generates $25,000/year forever at 5%. The principal is never touched.

Example 5: Car Loan Payment

Setup

You borrow $35,000 for a car at 6% annual rate, monthly payments, 5-year loan. What is your monthly payment?

Step 1: Identify Variables

PV = $35,000 (loan amount)  |  r = 6%/12 = 0.5% per month  |  n = 60 months

Step 2: Solve for PMT

PMT = PV × r ÷ [1 − (1+r)−n]

PMT = $35,000 × 0.005 ÷ [1 − (1.005)−60]

PMT = $175 ÷ [1 − 0.74137] = $175 ÷ 0.25863 = $676.65/month

Total paid: $676.65 × 60 = $40,599. Interest cost: $5,599.

Example 6: Retirement Savings (FV of Annuity)

Setup

You save $500/month at 9% annual return for 40 years. How much will you have at retirement?

Step 1: Identify Variables

PMT = $500/month  |  r = 9%/12 = 0.75% per month  |  n = 40 × 12 = 480 months

Step 2: Apply FV of Annuity Formula

FV = PMT × [(1+r)n − 1] / r

FV = $500 × [(1.0075)480 − 1] / 0.0075

FV = $500 × [36.1099 − 1] / 0.0075 = $500 × 4,681.32 = $2,340,659

Total deposited: $240,000. Compound growth: ~$2.1M!

Example 7: Should We Build the Factory? (NPV + IRR)

Setup

A factory costs $1,100,000 to build. It will generate $350,000/year in cash flows for 5 years. The company’s cost of capital is 12%. Should we build it?

Step 1: Calculate PV of Cash Flows

PV = $350,000 × [1 − (1.12)−5] / 0.12

PV = $350,000 × 3.60478 = $1,261,673

Step 2: Compute NPV

NPV = −$1,100,000 + $1,261,673 = +$161,673

NPV > 0 → Build the factory!

Step 3: Check IRR

IRR = 18.03% (the rate that makes NPV = 0)

IRR (18.03%) > Cost of Capital (12%) → Confirms: Accept!
