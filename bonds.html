Lecture 17 — Installment loans, bond pricing & amortization

L 17

How to Finance a Company

Every company needs capital. There are two fundamental ways to raise it:

Equity

  • Residual claim — equity holders are last in line, making it riskier for them
  • Can be relatively expensive because investors demand higher returns for bearing more risk
  • No obligation to repay — but owners share in upside and downside

Debt

  • More senior claims — debt holders are paid before equity holders
  • Can be long-term or short-term depending on the company's needs
  • Cash flow matching — companies match the duration of debt to the assets being financed

Types of Financial Liabilities

Private (Bank Loans)

  • Relationship lending — bank knows the borrower
  • Potential borrowing limits based on the bank's capacity
  • Negotiated terms, covenants, and flexibility

Public (Bonds)

  • More standardized terms for broad investor base
  • Enables large borrowings beyond what any single bank could provide
  • Cost efficient — competitive markets drive down borrowing costs

Installment Loans

Key features of installment loans:

Equal periodic payments, each comprising interest + principal repayment

The ratio changes each period: interest shrinks, principal grows

PV of all payments (annuity) = loan amount

Amortization Example

$500,000 loan, 9% annual interest, 5-year term with equal annual payments.

PV = PMT × [(1 − (1.09)−5) / 0.09]

PMT = $128,546

How each row works:

  • Opening × Rate = Interest
  • Payment − Interest = Principal
  • Opening − Principal = Closing

Loans Payable is a liability (L increases) when issued. Interest Expense reduces net income, which reduces Retained Earnings (SE decreases). Each payment reduces Cash (A decreases) and Loans Outstanding (L decreases). The equation stays balanced: A = L + SE.

What Are Debt Securities?

Bonds and notes are debt securities that trade in bond markets. They allow risk-sharing among a large pool of investors and enable companies to borrow very large amounts.

Key insight: Issuing a bond is equivalent to borrowing money. The company receives cash today and promises to make future payments (coupons + face value) to bondholders.

Key Features of a Bond

Face Value (Par Value)

The amount printed on the bond, repaid at maturity. This is the principal amount the issuer promises to return.

Coupon Rate

The interest rate stated on the bond. Determines the periodic cash payments to bondholders.

Maturity Date

When the principal (face value) is repaid to bondholders.

Issuer

The company (or government) borrowing the money by selling the bonds.

Two Types of Cash Flows

Periodic Coupon Payments

An annuity — usually semiannual

Coupon Rate × Face Value

Face Value at Maturity

A lump sum — returned at the end

Par Value

Zero-coupon bonds have no coupon payments — investors earn their return entirely from the discount between purchase price and face value at maturity.

A Tale of Two Interest Rates

Coupon Rate

Stated in the bond contract. Determines the cash paid to bondholders each period.

Cash Coupon = Face Value × Coupon Rate

Market Rate

The rate investors expect to earn. Determines the bond’s price.

Price = PV of coupons + PV of face value

Par, Premium, and Discount

At Par

Market Rate = Coupon Rate

Price = Face Value

At a Premium

Market Rate < Coupon Rate

Price > Face Value

Investors pay MORE because the coupon is generous

At a Discount

Market Rate > Coupon Rate

Price < Face Value

Investors pay LESS because the coupon is stingy

Bond Pricing

Running example: $400,000 face, 8% coupon, 5 years, semiannual payments. Semiannual coupon = $400,000 × 4% = $16,000, n = 10 periods.

Scenario Market Rate vs Coupon Rate Issue Price
At Par 8% = 8% $400,000
At Discount 10% > 8% $369,113
At Premium 6% < 8% $434,121

Issuing bonds increases Cash (A) and Bonds Payable (L). Bond Discount is a contra-liability (reduces L). Bond Premium increases L further. In all cases, A = L + SE stays balanced.

Discount Bond Interest

Cash Interest Paid + Amortization of Discount = Interest Expense

The discount is an additional cost to the issuer — you received less cash but must repay the full face value.

Effective Interest Amortization

Interest Expense = Net Book Value × Market Rate per period

Cash Paid = Face Value × Coupon Rate per period

Discount Amortization = Interest Expense − Cash Paid

New Net = Old Net + Amortization (net liability GROWS toward face value)

Bond Retirement (Discount)

Gain/Loss = Bonds Payable (net) − Repurchase Payment

  • If repurchase price < net book value → Gain
  • If repurchase price > net book value → Loss

Example: After Period 4, net = $379,697. Company calls bonds at 98 ($392,000).

Loss = $379,697 − $392,000 = −$12,303

Journal Entry:

Bonds Payable400,000
Loss on Retirement12,303
Bond Discount20,303
Cash392,000

GAAP: Part of ordinary income. Analysis: Omit from NOPAT (nonoperating).

Back to the Equation

Discount amortization increases the net liability (L increases) and increases Interest Expense (reducing SE). At retirement, a gain increases SE; a loss decreases SE. A = L + SE always holds.

Premium Bond Interest

Cash Interest Paid − Amortization of Premium = Interest Expense

The premium is a benefit to the issuer — you received extra cash upfront.

Effective Interest Amortization

Interest Expense = Net Book Value × Market Rate per period

Cash Paid = Face Value × Coupon Rate per period

Premium Amortization = Cash Paid − Interest Expense

New Net = Old Net − Amortization (net liability SHRINKS toward face value)

Bond Retirement (Premium)

ABC Company Example: $500,000 face, 8%, 20-year bonds issued at 106 ($30,000 premium). Premium amortized through Jan 1, 2016 = $5,000. Bonds retired at 103.

Premium remaining:

$30,000 − $5,000 = $25,000

Book value:

$500,000 + $25,000 = $525,000

Repurchase price:

$500,000 × 103% = $515,000

Gain:

$525,000 − $515,000 = $10,000

Journal Entry:

Bonds Payable500,000
Bond Premium25,000
Gain on Retirement10,000
Cash515,000

Back to the Equation

Premium amortization decreases the net liability (L decreases) and decreases Interest Expense (increasing SE relative to cash paid). A retirement gain increases SE; a loss decreases SE. A = L + SE always holds.

Example 3: Bond Retirement

$1,000,000 face, 7% coupon, issued at 103. Premium amortized so far = $8,000. Bonds retired at 98.

Step 1: Compute book value

Premium at issue = $1,000,000 × 3% = $30,000

Premium remaining = $30,000 − $8,000 = $22,000

Book Value = $1,000,000 + $22,000 = $1,022,000

Step 2: Compute repurchase price

Repurchase = $1,000,000 × 98% = $980,000

Step 3: Compute gain or loss

Gain = Book Value − Repurchase Price

Gain = $1,022,000 − $980,000 = $42,000

Step 4: Record journal entry

Bond Premium22,000
